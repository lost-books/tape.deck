Tob Gobble stepped down from the airplane into a wind that smelled of rain, hot metal, and distant snow. At the foot of the stairs, a representative of Matarax waited beneath the airport lights: a narrow, solemn figure in a dark coat, whose gloved hands were folded as if around an invisible thread.
“You have come far,” the representative said.
“Not so far as some,” Tob replied. He was small, rootleted, and difficult to place in any one century. “I once fled a stone roundhouse with less luggage.”
The representative offered no smile. Above them, the night seemed to have been stitched together with innumerable fine lines. Tob felt, rather than saw, the great spider’s design: prayers and passages woven into a web that reached beyond the world, through the old roads between planets and the buried routes beneath the ice.
The limousine waited at the curb. They drove in silence from the airport to Matarax headquarters, its dark windows reflecting the city in broken strips. Tob watched the buildings pass. Some bore the seals of corporations; others, the polished emblems of governments that had long ago become services one subscribed to and could cancel only at ruinous cost.
At last he said, “Does your master have a plan?”
“The web has many strands,” said the representative.
“That is not the same thing.”
“No.”
The headquarters rose from the ground like a black needle. Within, the halls were quiet and cold. In a chamber whose walls were lined with maps of Earth, Mars, and places without agreed names, Tob met Matarax—not the spider itself, but a presence gathered in a human outline, its stillness more unsettling than any threat.
They spoke of Dalton Trask: the magnate whose fortunes had been mined from asteroids, whose satellites kept watch over the world, whose algorithms offered government by subscription and whose hidden machines could make even a person’s memories seem negotiable. In one history, he had returned from exile to seize power. In another, he ruled Mars as its sole sovereign administrator, drawing its profits back to himself. The accounts disagreed on the details. They agreed on the danger.
“We will not meet force with force,” Matarax said. “We will turn his own dependencies against him. His empire is a chain of permissions, machines, and loyalties. Break the links, and the structure falls.”
“Quietly?” Tob asked.
“Subversively.”
“Ah.” Tob considered this. “Quietly is harder.”
They plotted through the night. Matarax would draw Trask’s attention toward a false disturbance in the satellite network. Tob would enter the empire’s buried records and find the one contradiction that could make its obedient systems doubt their master. The plan depended on secrecy, exact timing, and a door that had not been opened since the first Martian administrators mapped the routes between worlds.
Tob checked his watch.
Then he checked the watch on his second arm, and the third, and the fourth. Each displayed a different hour. One counted down. Another had stopped. A small dial on the last ticked backward with great conviction.
Matarax waited.
“Time is treacherous,” Tob said, fastening one strap. “It changes its story whenever it thinks no one is listening.”
Their operation began before dawn. Matarax sent its false disturbance across the web of satellites, drawing Trask’s attention outward, toward a threat that seemed to approach from the empty reaches beyond Earth. Meanwhile, Tob slipped into the headquarters’ old passageways, following a thread of prayer through the walls.
The passage ended at a door beneath the foundations. Beyond it lay a tunnel said to connect worlds: once a road for the People of Anthuor, later a route through Hypogeic space, and—according to other accounts—a subterranean path down from the UFO caves in Antarctica. The histories did not fit together. The tunnel did not object.
Tob entered.
On the other side, he found the buried computer and the records Matarax had promised. They were not a single account of Trask’s rule but many: incompatible versions of his rise, his exile, his machines, his victories. The systems that maintained his authority had been instructed to accept one official history. Tob offered them all the others.
For a moment, the empire hesitated.
Then the alarms sounded.
Trask’s voice came through the speakers, smooth with practiced certainty. “Whoever is interfering with my systems, understand that every version of this world ends with me in control.”
“That sounds lonely,” Tob said.
The door behind him opened. Trask stood beyond it, or an image of him did. His face flickered between ages and expressions, as though reality itself were uncertain which Dalton Trask it had been ordered to display. For an instant, Tob saw another figure inside that image: a crowned hunter, a spirit departing, a body collapsing in a place of decay. Then the vision broke apart.
Matarax’s web tightened. Across the network, permissions failed; satellites went dark one by one, and the buried computer began returning the empire’s own contradictory histories to every terminal it controlled. Trask’s image vanished from the screen.
Nothing else did.
The alarms continued. The tunnel shuddered. Far above, the first light of morning touched the glass towers, while beneath them the old road between worlds began to close.
Tob checked his watch again. The backward dial had stopped.
“That,” he said, “is never a good sign.”
Matarax reached for the thread leading back to the headquarters. They had opened a crack in Trask’s empire, not toppled it. And now the web carried a new signal from beyond the world: a prayer, or an answer, moving along the same strand they had used to strike.
Tob followed Matarax into the dark. The plan had gone wrong. The next part, as ever, belonged to whoever was still moving.
